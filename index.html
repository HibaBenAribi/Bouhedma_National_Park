Bouhedma
National Park

Tunisia · UNESCO Biosphere Reserve

The last savanna in North Africa

The park was created in 1980 for two purposes: conservation — keeping the savanna, its water points and its remaining wild populations intact — and reintroduction — returning the species that were exterminated in the wild, starting with the scimitar-horned oryx and the dama gazelle.

Human–wildlife coexistence

The park is zoned so that every user of the landscape has a dedicated space: a closed core for pure conservation, fenced reintroduction grounds, seasonal grazing areas for local communities, and corridor zones where gazelles, oryx and hyenas move between the plain and the djebel without crossing villages. The scientific principle is simple — separate people and wildlife in space and time, then connect the wild spaces to each other, and monitor the shared points (water, pasture, passages) with cameras and counts.

Conservation effort

The scimitar-horned oryx, the Barbary sheep, and the ostrich once lived across this steppe. Hunting and drought eliminated them from Tunisia — the oryx disappeared from the wild entirely, the Barbary sheep and the ostrich from the Tunisian landscape. Bouhedma brought all three back: the oryx from captive stock into fenced then open ranges, the Barbary sheep to the rocks of Djebel Bouhedma, where a managed reproduction enclosure now supports the wild population, and the ostrich to the open plain.

Biodiversity

The park shelters 227 species of flora and fauna — wild populations and reintroduced ones that are now breeding again.

Research

Bouhedma is a hub for research on reintroduction and coexistence. Modern technologies are used by scientists to study and monitor the animal populations in the park.
