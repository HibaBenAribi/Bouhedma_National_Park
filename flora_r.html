1. Introduction & Botanical Context

Phytoecological surveys in arid ecosystems provide critical baseline data for assessing plant biomass and species richness. During field training at Bouhedma National Park with WWF North Africa, botanical monitoring focuses on evaluating plant distribution across varying topography, tracking seasonal phenology, and recording structural measurements using standardized quantitative methods.

2. Geological Zonation & Habitat Diversity

Bouhedma National Park exhibits distinct geological formations and hydrological gradients, creating micro-habitats that host specialized plant assemblages unique to each zone:

A. Flat Savanna Plains

Alluvial plains dominated by pre-Saharan Vachellia tortilis (Acacia) savanna, Stipa tenacissima, and drought-resistant halophytic shrubs.

B. Mountain & Jebel Slopes

Rocky limestone/gypsaceous slopes (Jebel Bouhedma) hosting rupicolous species, relict trees like Juniperus phoenicea, and specialized chasmophytes.

C. Water Source Corridors

Riparian wadi beds and spring outlets where moisture availability supports dense vegetation (Retama raetam, Pistacia atlantica, and perennial grasses).

Field Sampling Locations: Sampling must be repeated across these distinct geological zones—from the open flat plains near camp to the elevated mountain slopes and humid spring corridors—to capture the full phytodiversity of the park.

3. Sampling Method 1: The Nested Quadrat Technique

The nested quadrat technique determines the minimal area required to sample a plant community effectively. The method begins with a small sub-frame and progressively expands outward, recording all newly encountered plant species at each increment.

Nested Quadrat Expansion Diagram

Figure 1. Concentric nested quadrat expansion from $0.5\text{m} \times 0.5\text{m}$ ($0.25\text{m}^2$) up to $10\text{m} \times 10\text{m}$ ($100\text{m}^2$).

Step-by-Step Expansion Protocol:

  1. Initial Frame (0.5m × 0.5m): Place a square frame divided into smaller grids. Identify and record all rooted plant species present.
  2. Progressive Scaling: Expand the boundary systematically to 1m × 1m, 2m × 2m, 4m × 4m, up to 10m × 10m (or the maximum plot size for the habitat).
  3. Record New Species: At each stage, note only newly encountered species and record photographic reference data. Stop expanding when no new species are added (reaching the asymptote of the species-area curve).

4. Sampling Method 2: Fixed Line-Transect & Offset Dendrometry

This quantitative method measures individual plant structural attributes (dendrometry) along a precise, linear corridor.

Field Layout & Measurement Rules:

  • 3-Stake Alignment: Place 3 reference stakes in the ground spaced exactly 0.5m apart. Attach a 10m measuring tape to the central stake to establish the main transect line.
  • 0.5m Corridor Limit: Walk along the 10m line and inspect rooted perennial plants. Only count plants whose main stem/root system falls within ≤ 0.5m perpendicular distance from the central tape. Plants located beyond 0.5m are excluded.
  • Dendrometric Measurements: For each qualified plant, record:
    • Total Height (H): Vertical height in meters from root base to apex.
    • Crown Diameters (D1 & D2): Crown canopy width along the widest axis (D1) and perpendicular axis (D2) in centimeters.
    • Base Diameter: Trunk/stem diameter at ground level in centimeters.

5. Seasonal Dynamics & Identification Timing

In arid North African ecosystems, plant visibility varies drastically across the year. Many annual species remain dormant in the seed bank during dry months, while geophytes and deciduous shrubs drop leaves or desiccate completely.

A. Spring Survey Window (March – May)

Peak flowering and vegetative growth. Essential for identifying ephemeral annuals, grasses, and flowering diagnostic parts required for taxonomic keys.

B. Autumn Survey Window (September – November)

Post-first rains germination phase. Critical for evaluating perennial woodiness, seed germination, and post-summer survival rates.

Multi-Session Identification Rule:

Plants that appear dead or unidentifiable during one season may be vital living components in another. Complete phytodiversity inventories require visiting the exact same plots across both spring and autumn sessions, recording species codes on standard field data sheets (*Fiche de Relevé Phytoécologique*).
